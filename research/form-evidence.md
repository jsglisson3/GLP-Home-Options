# What the contact form is built on: evidence and judgment calls

**Written:** Oct 26, 2026 (Checkpoint 46). Desk research only: nothing was submitted, downloaded or run. Where a source is weak, it is marked. Where the form goes against a source, that is said too. Text-message rules and the questions for an attorney are **not** here; they are in `compliance-questions-for-joey.md` (private, for the family and an attorney).

**Strength:** A = standards body or peer-reviewed. B = respected UX group, government design system or platform documentation. C = vendor blog, forum or self-reported number (weak). "My inference" means no source says it; I reasoned it out.

## 1. How many fields, and how many steps

1. One page; name and phone required, everything else optional. Baymard's benchmark: checkouts average about 11 fields and about 8 would do (e-commerce, not lead forms). https://baymard.com/blog/checkout-flow-average-form-fields (B)
2. Do not split about five fields into steps: Baymard finds neither one-page nor multi-step wins; the effort of each step is what counts. https://baymard.com/blog/one-page-checkout (B). NN/g favours wizards for rare or unfamiliar tasks but gives no length threshold. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/wizards/ (B)
3. Skip progress bars: a 32-experiment meta-analysis found constant indicators did not significantly reduce drop-off (surveys). https://research.google/pubs/where-am-i-a-meta-analysis-of-experiments-on-the-effects-of-progress-indicators-for-web-surveys/ (A)
4. Put at most one low-value optional field behind a short, descriptive disclosure link, and never hide what most people need. https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/details (B). Baymard also endorses collapsing rarely used optional fields and marking both kinds explicitly. https://baymard.com/blog/required-optional-form-fields (B). Mark optional fields "(optional)", not with asterisks. https://design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/question-pages (B)
5. Peer-reviewed support is thin: 20 form guidelines applied together improved speed and satisfaction (N=65, CHI 2014), not field count specifically. https://research.google/pubs/designing-usable-web-forms-empirical-evaluation-of-web-form-improvement-guidelines/ (A). I found no controlled field-count study for short lead forms. HubSpot's 40,000-page analysis (C) says each added field costs a little, and a free-text box costs more; no numbers. An "86% better" multi-step claim (C) cites unnamed sources and its own averages do not match it, so it was not used.

**In the form:** one page; name and phone are the only required fields; the first screen on a phone shows the heading, Call and Text, then the form's start. "(optional)" is written out, no asterisks; no progress bar. **Where it goes past the source:** four optional things (email, best way to reach you, address, a note) sit behind one disclosure, "Add an email, your address, or a note", not one. The reason is the first screen on a small phone, and the disclosure opens by itself if the email is wrong. The one-tap topic buttons stay out in the open because they are the cheapest thing to answer. If real use shows people never open the disclosure, or that they want one of those boxes up front, that is a one-line change; there is no data yet.

## 2. Phones: keyboards, autofill, touch

1. One name field with `autocomplete="name"` (Baymard: 42% of testers typed a full name into "First Name"). https://baymard.com/blog/checkout-flow-average-form-fields (B). Email `type="email" autocomplete="email" spellcheck="false"`. Phone `type="tel" autocomplete="tel"`, the full number (`tel-national` and the like are for split fields). https://developer.mozilla.org/docs/Web/HTML/Attributes/autocomplete (B); https://design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/telephone-numbers (B)
2. Street `address-line1` on a one-line input. Never `type="number"` for a ZIP. https://web.dev/articles/payment-and-address-form-best-practices (B); `inputmode` only changes the keyboard. https://developer.mozilla.org/docs/Web/HTML/Reference/Global_attributes/inputmode (B)
3. Valid autocomplete tokens satisfy WCAG 1.3.5 for fields about the user. https://w3c.github.io/wcag/understanding/identify-input-purpose.html (A)
4. Visible labels, never placeholder-only. WCAG 3.3.2 https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/labels-or-instructions.html (A); NN/g: placeholders vanish and make fixing errors harder https://www.nngroup.com/articles/form-design-placeholders/ (B); Baymard: mobile users deleted entries to see hidden labels https://baymard.com/blog/mobile-forms-avoid-inline-labels (B).
5. Touch targets: WCAG 2.5.8 AA is 24 by 24 CSS px (A); 2.5.5 AAA is 44 by 44 (A) https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/target-size-enhanced.html ; Apple 44 pt (B); Android 48 dp (B). I chose 52 px or taller for boxes, tick-buttons and buttons (the main buttons are 60 px) and 44 px for the small links (my synthesis).
6. Text at 16 px or more in boxes: iOS Safari zooms the page on focus below that (reported by CSS-Tricks, C; no Apple or WebKit page found). Do not fix it with `user-scalable=no`, which fails WCAG 1.4.4. https://dequeuniversity.com/rules/axe/4.9/meta-viewport (B)
7. Phone numbers: accept any way of writing one; tidy it when the person leaves the box; no per-keystroke mask. GOV.UK https://design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/telephone-numbers (B); MDN https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/HTML/Element/input/tel (B); Baymard: mask only if flawless, since 89% of testers typed a different format than the one shown. https://baymard.com/research-articles/input-masking-form-field (B)
8. If you do format while typing (my inference): act only on typed characters (never on deleting), count the digits left of the cursor, and leave the box alone whenever you are unsure. https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Input.setSelectionRange (B)

**In the form:** all of 1 to 4 and 6; the boxes are 54 px tall at 1.05 rem. **Where it goes past the source:** it formats the number as it is typed, which the sources discourage. It is the careful version: it only formats when the finger is at the end of the box, does nothing while deleting, while a keyboard is still composing, with a leading 1, 0 or +, or past ten digits, and it tidies any way of writing a US number when the person leaves the box. The reason is that older visitors like to see the number take shape (it is proof the box heard them). It has been tested by script; it has **not** been tried on real Android and iPhone keyboards. If it ever misbehaves, deleting the `input` handler in `js/form.js` (section 2) leaves the tidy-on-leaving behaviour, which is the source-approved version.

## 3. Mistakes and messages

1. Check when Send is pressed, and clear each message the moment it is fixed. Sources disagree about checking when a box is left: GOV.UK says not on leaving (B); NN/g warns against flagging too early (B); USWDS shows errors only after the person has interacted (B); Baymard says check on leaving, then on every key (B). https://design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/validation ; https://www.nngroup.com/articles/error-message-guidelines/ ; https://designsystem.digital.gov/components/text-input/ ; https://baymard.com/research-articles/inline-form-validation
2. The pattern: one box at the top that gets the focus and links to each field; a message above each box with a hidden "Error:" first; `aria-describedby` to the hint and the message; the same words in both places; keep what the person typed. https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/error-summary ; https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/error-message (B)
3. `aria-invalid="true"` only after checking has run. https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Techniques/aria/ARIA21 (A)
4. Do not rely on live-region announcements for inline status: USWDS is retiring its validation component because screen-reader and magnifier users missed the changes (B); GOV.UK moved `role="alert"` to an inner element after JAWS skipped the summary text (B).
5. Say what is wrong and how to fix it, in plain words, without "invalid" or "please". https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/error-message ; https://www.nngroup.com/articles/error-message-guidelines/ (B)
6. WCAG 3.3.1 (say it in text, not only colour), 3.3.3 (suggest the fix when it is known), 3.3.7 (do not ask people to type the same thing twice, so no "confirm your email" box) (A). https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/error-identification ; https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/error-suggestion.html ; https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/redundant-entry.html

**In the form:** all of it. Messages: "Please tell us your name." / "Please add a phone number so we can reach you." / "That phone number looks too short. Please include the area code, like (423) 555-0123." / "That email address doesn't look right. It should look like name@example.com." (most of them use "please", which the source says to avoid; on a form for nervous older homeowners I kept the softer word. The wording is easy to change.) A box that has something wrong in it is told when the person leaves it; an empty one is not scolded until Send is pressed. The box at the top follows the boxes below it: a line changes with its message and goes the moment its problem is fixed, and the box goes with the last line, so it never keeps listing something already put right. It is announced once, when it appears; after its first change it stops being a live region, so what changes while someone is typing never interrupts them. A phone keyboard's "next" key sends Enter: in the name, email and address boxes (each followed by another box) it moves on to the next box instead of sending the form. A box with the keyboard in it shows a solid 3 px navy outline as well as the green border and soft glow (a review found the glow alone was too faint, and that the note box had no outline at all).

## 4. Trust and expectations

1. Real photos and first names of both owners beside the form, no stock people. NN/g eye-tracking examples (2010, sample not reported): staff photos were studied, stock models ignored. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/photos-as-web-content/ (B). Stanford's credibility guidelines (studies of 4,500+ people, 1999 to 2002, dated): show real people, an address, easy contact. https://credibility.stanford.edu/guidelines (A/B)
2. Say upfront what you are asking for; NN/g reports that asking for personal data before showing value damages trust. https://nngroup.com/articles/trustworthy-design/ (B)
3. Say why you want a phone number. Baymard: 39% of sites ask with no explanation, and 14% of 1,026 shoppers would never give one. https://baymard.com/blog/explain-phone-number-field (B)
4. Say what happens next: who contacts them, and that nothing is owed. Pattern basis only: GOV.UK confirmation pages. https://design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/confirmation-pages (B)
5. Name the outcome on the button, not "Submit". NN/g prefers specific verbs https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ui-copy/ (B); GOV.UK button text describes the action https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/button/ (B). Vendor A/B lifts were left out as self-reported.
6. No countdowns or "only 2 slots left". A 60-second timer plus "act now" did not significantly raise acceptance, and aggressive tactics caused anger and dropouts (N=1,963 and 3,777). https://academic.oup.com/jla/article/13/1/43/6180579 (A). The FTC's staff list false urgency and scarcity as dark patterns. https://www.ftc.gov/reports/bringing-dark-patterns-light (B, not law). None of these studied home-seller forms; the link to trust is inference.
7. One plain sentence about who sees the data and that it is used only for this request (basis: items 2 and 3).

**In the form:** the heading is a question ("Thinking about selling, or not sure yet?"); Joey and Ethan's photos and first names sit at the top of the card; under the heading, "Joey or Ethan will get back to you. There's no obligation, and only your name and phone number are required."; under the phone box, "So Joey or Ethan can reach you about your request."; the main button says "Get my message ready" while nothing is connected (it sends nothing, and says so) and "Send to Joey & Ethan" once a receiving end exists (the builder picks the words); under it, "No obligation, no spam. Your information is kept confidential." (carried over from the old form); and the thank-you says "We have your details, and Joey or Ethan will be in touch." **No time is promised anywhere**, on purpose: a time goes on the site only when Joey says he can keep it every time. No countdown, no scarcity, no pre-ticked box.

## 5. Text and email links ("sms:" and "mailto:")

1. RFC 5724: `sms:+14235550100?body=hello%20there`; at least one recipient; fields joined by `&`; `body` at most once, percent-encoded UTF-8; the person can edit, and sending should need their confirmation. Silent on line breaks; the user agent decides limits. https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc5724 (A)
2. Phones differ. Apple's archived SMS-link page (2017) documents `sms:<number>` and no message text, so a pre-filled body on iPhone is unofficial. https://developer.apple.com/library/ios/featuredarticles/iPhoneURLScheme_Reference/SMSLinks/SMSLinks.html (B). Libraries and blogs report `?body=` on Android, `&body=` on iOS 8 and later. https://npmjs.com/package/sms-href (C); https://weblog.west-wind.com/posts/2013/Oct/09/Prefilling-an-SMS-on-Mobile-Devices-with-the-sms-Uri-Scheme (C)
3. `sms:+14235550100?&body=ENCODED` is reported to work on both (a vendor help page and a university page, 2021 at the latest, C). It is not valid by the RFC (an empty first field), and one library warns that combined separators fail on some devices. https://help.blooio.com/how-to-create-sms-hyperlinks-with-pre-filled-messages ; https://communications.uams.edu/web/kb/creating-an-sms-link (C)
4. Encode with `encodeURIComponent`; spaces must be `%20`. Keep curly quotes, dashes and emoji out of a text message (they push it into the more expensive 70-character kind).
5. RFC 6068 for `mailto:`: first header after `?`, later ones after `&`; plain-text body; line breaks must be `%0D%0A`; encode `?`, `=` and `&`; no length limit stated; the mail program shows the message for approval. https://rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6068 (A). iOS 14.6 Mail reportedly showed `%0D%0A` as a literal `<BR>` (a 2021 thread, no fix reported). https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/681023 (C)
6. On a desktop computer, RFC 5724 lets the program decide what to open, and I could not confirm what happens. So: always show the number as text next to a Copy button, and put the email button first on a computer and the text button first on a phone. The script counts a device as a phone only when its pointer is coarse **and** its screen is small (under 500 px on the short side): an iPad is a touch screen that cannot send a text by itself, so it gets the email button first, like a computer. https://developer.mozilla.org/docs/Web/CSS/@media/pointer (B)
7. Copying: `navigator.clipboard.writeText` inside the click, on HTTPS only, with the failure handled; the fallback is a hidden textarea and `document.execCommand('copy')` (deprecated), and the last resort is text the person can press and hold. https://developer.mozilla.org/en-us/docs/web/api/clipboard_api ; https://web.dev/articles/async-clipboard (B)

**In the form:** `sms:+14234515707?&body=…`, with the form's own sentences in plain characters (curly quotes and long dashes the visitor types become plain ones); an email with the subject "Website request from NAME - TOPIC" and the line breaks as `%0D%0A`; text button first only on a phone-sized touch screen, email first on a computer or a tablet (neither can be counted on to send a text), and a phone visitor who chose Email under "Best way to reach you" gets email first too (what someone prefers for the reply is not what the device can send, so a computer visitor who chose Text still gets the email button first); the words of the two buttons follow the order ("Text it to Joey & Ethan" / "Or email it instead", or "Email it to Joey & Ethan" / "Or text it instead"); a Copy button and the phone number always on the panel; the message is marked "do not translate", so a browser's translator cannot change what is about to be sent; the page says "Nothing has been sent yet" until the person's own app sends it, and when they come back to the page it says "Back already? If your message went through, Joey and Ethan have it. If not, try the button again, or call us." (it cannot know whether they pressed Send). Any click in the panel in its first moments is ignored, so the second click of a double-click on Send cannot press a button that has just appeared.


## Two independent reviews of the code, and what became of each finding

Two fresh readers, who had not seen how the form was built, read the finished code and its markup: the first before the last round of fixes, the second after it. They are listed here so the next person can see what was found, what was done, and what was left alone on purpose. Nothing here is legal advice.

**Review 1: 2 blockers, 9 important and 13 minor findings.**

| Finding | What happened |
|---|---|
| B1. A filled hidden "Website" box showed a false "Thank you" and sent nothing, in both modes | **Fixed, and the design changed.** The page no longer judges the box. In hand-off mode it is ignored; with a receiver it is posted (with `seconds_on_form`) only when filled, and the receiver flags it. There is no fake thank-you anywhere. |
| B2. Send pressed before the form's script had run did an ordinary page request, which put the visitor's details in the address bar | **Fixed.** The form tag carries `method="post"` and a one-line inline handler that cancels a native send; a test presses Send with the script blocked. |
| I1. The two text-message ticks went nowhere while nothing records them | **Fixed (one of the reviewer's two options).** A ticked box becomes a line in the ready-made message; the sentences themselves are untouched. |
| I2. An iPhone's "next" key sent the whole form | **Fixed.** "Next" moves to the next box. |
| I3. The text boxes had almost no visible focus (1.2:1) | **Fixed.** The site's 3 px ring; a test checks it. |
| I4. The 6-second timer could swap a working form for the call card for good (slow connection, or an unrelated error in `site.js`) | **Half fixed, on purpose.** If the form's script arrives after the timer, the form comes back (tested with a throttled load). The timer still fires when `site.js` itself crashes, because the form shares the page's `js` switch with the rest of the page; the call card then shows, which is the safe side. |
| I5. Tablets, and computers where "Text" was chosen, got a text button first, which often cannot be delivered | **Fixed.** Only a phone-sized touch screen gets the text button first. |
| I6. The hand-off words contradicted themselves ("Send" that did not send; "Email it instead" with nothing before it) | **Fixed.** "Get my message ready" until a receiver exists; the two button labels follow the order they are shown in. |
| I7. Real international numbers and extensions were refused | **Mostly fixed.** International numbers are kept as typed. An extension is not parsed; the error says it can go in the note. |
| I8. A post that was delivered could be reported as "We couldn't send that" (no cross-origin header, a slow answer) | **Fixed in words and behaviour.** The heading is "We couldn't confirm that was sent"; the post uses `keepalive`; the fallback uses the details as they are now. Not done: a random reference on every send to match duplicates (named in `form-wiring.md` as an option if duplicates turn out to matter). |
| I9. The receiver never saw the trap, and a planned time filter would have dropped real people | **Fixed with B1.** The receiver is told to flag, never to drop, and `seconds_on_form` is only a hint. |
| M1, M2, M4, M6, M7, M8, M9, M10, M12: a lone surrogate character making Send do nothing; the error box too low at high zoom; "Thank you, Mr."; impossible US numbers accepted; the alert role on the box that takes focus and the email link when the fold is closed; a screen reader hearing only the panel's name; Copy taking translated text; a double click hitting what the first click uncovered; edits during "Sending" | **Fixed**, each with a test or a screenshot. |
| M3. Long text is cut off silently (note 600, name 80 characters) | **Left alone on purpose.** The limits are well above what people type and the note's hint says "Up to 600 characters". Revisit with real use. |
| M5. One-character names and full-width or Arabic-Indic digits are refused | **Left alone on purpose.** Rare, and the error says what to do. |
| M11. The browser's Back button leaves the page from the message panel | **Left alone.** Adding history entries to a one-page form is more likely to confuse than help, and "Change my details" is on the panel. |
| M13. "Call or text anytime" reads as an availability promise | **Left alone here; already tracked** (`CONTENT_CLAIMS.md` row 5; questions 3 and 19 in `questions-for-joey.md`). |

**Review 2: 0 blockers, 1 important and 8 minor findings.** The second reader ran the real `js/form.js` against the real form markup in a small imitation of a page (not a browser), then read the markup, the styles and the builder. Nothing in the main path broke: name and phone, Send, the message screen, the text and email buttons, Change my details and Copy all worked in every case played. Every fix below has a test in `tools/form-test.py` that fails on the earlier script and passes on the new one (run against both to be sure: 11 failures on the old script, none on the new).

| Finding | What happened |
|---|---|
| I-1. Pressing the phone keyboard's Done key (or Enter) in a wrong or empty phone box took the alert role off the error box in the same instant the box appeared, so a screen reader might not announce it | **Fixed.** One line in the function that keeps the box in step with the fields: when the words have not changed, nothing is touched. Three cases tested in a real browser. What a screen reader then says still needs a real device (VoiceOver, TalkBack, NVDA). |
| M-A. Two or more half-emoji in a row (a broken paste) still made the link-building throw, and the form disappeared behind the call card | **Fixed.** A whole emoji is kept; any other half becomes the replacement mark. |
| M-B. Enter on one of the choices or on a text-message box sent the form | **Fixed.** Enter does nothing there; Space still ticks. |
| M-C. A held Enter key moved to the phone box and then sent the form from it | **Fixed.** Repeats of a held key are ignored in the boxes. |
| M-D. The double-click guard counted a clock set back as "just now" (every button of the message screen dead until the time caught up), and covered only the card (on a phone the second click could land on the tiles below it) | **Fixed both.** A negative time is ignored, and the guard now covers the whole page for the first 0.45 seconds of a screen. The tile case is tested by script; a real double tap on a real phone is still worth one look. |
| M-E. "Press the green button" named a colour, and nothing said what to do when no mail app opens (common on computers that use webmail) | **Fixed in the words** (the exact words are Jeremy's and Joey's to change): the line names the button ("Press “Text it to Joey & Ethan”" or "Press “Email it to Joey & Ethan”") and ends "If nothing opens, press “Copy my message” below and paste it into a text (or an email), or call us." A test checks that the line names the button actually shown first. |
| M-F. The older copy method (browsers with no clipboard) dropped the keyboard's place | **Fixed.** The keyboard goes back to the Copy button. |
| M-G. If the receiving end failed after the visitor had emptied a box, the hand-off message could read "this is ." | **Fixed.** The details that passed the check are used. |
| M-H. The lines in the error box were about 21 px tall to tap | **Fixed.** 44 px; the size test now measures them at every width. |
| Not changed: the first press on Send right after leaving a half-typed phone number might be lost, because the small error under the box moves the page between pressing and releasing | **Left alone; it needs a real phone to see whether it happens.** The error is already on screen when it does, and a second press works. If it turns out to matter, skip that check when the next thing pressed is the Send button. |

## What I could not confirm (needs a real phone, or a real receiver)

- Apple documents no pre-filled `sms:` body; the `?&body=` form rests on two vendor or university pages with no list of devices. **Test on real iPhone and Android phones before launch.**
- How line breaks behave in a text message body; the longest safe `sms:` or `mailto:` address on iOS, Android and Outlook; what `sms:` does on a computer; whether iOS Mail still mishandles `%0D%0A`.
- The 16 px zoom threshold on iOS (no Apple or WebKit documentation).
- What real screen readers (VoiceOver, TalkBack, NVDA, JAWS) say when the box with the problems appears, when the message panel opens (it is described by the line that says nothing has been sent yet) and when the box changes. The markup follows the sources above and axe finds nothing, but nobody has listened to it.
- Whether the page jumps when the box at the top loses its last line while a phone keyboard is open (Safari has no scroll anchoring). The same kind of shift already happens when an inline message clears, and the box is only ever above the boxes being typed in.
- Whether a receiving end (GoHighLevel's inbound webhook, or anything else) answers cross-origin posts. See `research/form-wiring.md`.
- No controlled evidence was found for privacy wording, button labels, owner photos or "what happens next" blocks on seller lead forms; those choices rest on the general UX sources above and on this site's own rules (`DESIGN_SYSTEM.md`, sections 0 and 8).
- NN/g's progressive-disclosure article was rate-limited when read, and the Fogg 2001 paper page needed JavaScript, so Stanford's summary stands in for it.
