# Review kit: how GLP asks for reviews honestly, and how the site shows them

**Written:** Oct 25–26, 2026, overnight (Checkpoint 44). **For:** Joey and Ethan, with Jeremy.
**Status: DRAFT. Nothing in this file has been sent, posted or turned on.** There is no Google listing yet and no reviews (the Facebook page says "Not yet rated"). The site shows no stars, counts or review quotes anywhere, and it will not until real ones exist.
**Not legal advice.** Items marked **ATTORNEY** need a Tennessee attorney's glance first. Platform and regulator pages are quoted as they read on Oct 25, 2026 (an assistant read them through a tool that returns an extraction, so check the wording on the page before relying on it). Numbers from software vendors and surveys are labeled as that. Sources are at the end; the private research report has the long version.

## The short version

1. **Ask everyone who closed with you, the same way,** a few days after the money has moved. Never choose who to ask by how you think the sale went.
2. **Give nothing in return.** No gift card, discount, entry or favor. Do not suggest words, a star rating, a topic or a name to mention.
3. **Joey, Ethan, their families and anyone who works for GLP do not post reviews** of GLP on Google.
4. **One link, one reminder, then stop.** Use the link or QR code from the Google Business Profile itself.
5. **Expect about one review a month or fewer.** A slow, steady trickle is normal, and local-search experts rank "a sustained influx of reviews over time (rather than bursts)" 14th among the map-result factors they scored. Plan in quarters, not weeks.
6. **The site stays quiet until there is something real to show.** The switch is one file and a set of checks that refuse anything that is not real. See "Turning it on" near the end.

## Why it matters, in two lines

Reviews are one of the things people read before choosing a local company. In a 2026 survey (a software vendor's, 1,002 US adults) 47% said they would not use a business with fewer than 20 reviews, and 74% only consider reviews from the last three months. And the rules are real: the FTC's consumer review rule has been in force since Oct 21, 2024, and its December 2025 warning-letter template cites civil penalties of up to $53,088 per violation. Asking real customers is fine. Bought or fake reviews, asking only the happy customers, and hiding negative reviews while implying the ones shown are all of them are not.

## The rules, in plain words

| Rule | Why (short) |
|---|---|
| Ask every customer the same way. Do not ask only the happy ones. | Google bans "selectively solicit[ing] positive reviews" (called review gating). The FTC's staff say asking only satisfied customers "could violate the FTC Act". |
| Nothing in return, ever. | Google: incentives to post, change or remove a review are "strictly prohibited". BBB and Trustpilot ban them too. The FTC does not ban every incentive, but the platforms are stricter, so GLP follows the platforms. A thank-you note is fine; a gift tied to a review is not. |
| Do not script it: no star rating, no topic, no staff name, no "mention the fast close". | Google's policy (as read Oct 25, 2026) bars asking staff to collect reviews with specific content, such as a staff member's name, and bars steering reviews toward the positive. Practitioners disagree about how far that reaches, so GLP stays well inside it. If a customer asks "what should I write?", the answer is "whatever is true for you". |
| No reviews by owners, relatives, employees or business partners. | Google treats them as conflicts of interest, and the FTC requires such reviews to be disclosed. Do not invite them. |
| Never ask anyone to change or delete a review, and never offer anything for it. | Google bans incentives to change or remove; the FTC says asking people to change or delete negative reviews "could mislead readers". |
| No asking at the closing table, and nothing while any part of the deal is open. | Google bars pressure to leave a review while on the premises. Waiting a few days also keeps the request clear of any appearance of leverage. |
| Do not tell reviewers to include a phone number or website. | Google does not allow them inside reviews. |
| Never buy reviews, followers or likes. | FTC rule, and Google. |

## Who to ask

- **The rule (decide it once, write it down, use it every time):** everyone who closed a deal with us, who has agreed to hear from us afterward.
- The only reasons to leave someone off the list are the rule's own: they have not agreed to be contacted (or asked not to be), or something about the deal is still open. **Never** leave someone off because you think they were unhappy.
- **ATTORNEY:** whether to hold back people with an open dispute, and whether to include people who received an offer but did not sell. This kit's default is closed deals only.
- Back catalog (past sellers): the same message, **a handful per week**, not all at once. Google's filter looks for "unusual volumes or patterns" and does not say what that means; experts disagree on whether a burst triggers it, so the cautious choice is a trickle.
- Keep a simple private log (template below) as proof of equal treatment. It is a record, not a quota.

## When to ask

- **At closing:** say one sentence, no ask: "Thank you for trusting us with this. In a few days I'll send you a short note with a link, in case you ever want to share how it went. It's completely optional." A printed card in the closing folder is fine (below).
- **3 to 7 days after the seller has been paid:** send the message (below). This is our recommendation, not a Google rule.
- **One reminder, 7 to 10 days later,** to anyone who has not told you they already did it. Then stop. Same timing for everyone.

## How to ask: three ways

1. **A printed card with a QR code.** Needs no phone consent, which makes it the simplest start. Google itself suggests a printed QR code. In the Google Business Profile: Read reviews → "Get more reviews" (the share icon) → copy the link or download the QR code (the QR code can only be made in a desktop browser). Suggested card text: *"Thank you for trusting us with your home. If you'd like to share your experience, scan this code. It's optional, and an honest review of any kind is welcome."* **Do not print cards until the link exists and has been tested on a phone** (see "The link" below). Any print shop or free QR maker will print the code Google gives you; check the finished card with a real phone before printing a stack.
2. **Email,** sent from info@glphomeoptions.com, with the message below. **ATTORNEY:** I did not research email rules (CAN-SPAM) for a request like this.
3. **Text.** One software vendor's data suggests texts get more replies than email (about 20 reviews per 100 text requests against 15 per 100 emails; vendor figures, not about real estate). But texting is the channel with legal strings, and **this kit leaves it out until the questions below are answered.** The one-to-one text in `research/joey-kit.md` (section 1) stays a draft until then.

### The texting questions (ATTORNEY, before any review text goes out)

- Does a review request count as **marketing** or as an **informational** message? One vendor calls it "legally unsettled" and advises using the stricter standard. That decides what kind of consent is needed.
- The website form's consent wording lives inside GoHighLevel and could not be read. The Terms list inquiry messages, reminders and follow-ups; a review request after closing is not listed. Cleaner path: a short separate consent line at or before closing ("may we text you after closing about your experience?"), with the answer recorded. Exact wording is the attorney's call.
- A new FCC order (FCC 26-67, adopted Sept 30, 2026) changes how opt-outs work. It was published in the Federal Register on Oct 26, 2026 (91 FR 67214) and takes effect Nov 25, 2026. Its Federal Register summary says callers may name an exclusive way to opt out and may read an opt-out as covering only the category of informational messages it was sent in; an attorney should read the order before any text program is built. Until it takes effect, the current rule applies: honor any reasonable "stop" and put "Reply STOP to opt out" in every text.
- As a precaution, send texts only between 8 a.m. and 9 p.m. in the recipient's local time. (The federal quiet-hours limit, 47 CFR 64.1200(c)(1), is written for "telephone solicitations"; whether a post-closing review request is one is an attorney question.) If texts go through a registered texting program, its registered use should include review requests.
- Whether a one-to-one text typed from Joey's own phone is treated differently from one sent by a platform, and whether the Tennessee Do Not Call/Text register applies. (Details are in the gitignored `research/compliance-questions-for-joey.md`.)

## The wording (drafts; put them in Joey's voice)

**The message (email or, once cleared, text), day 3 to 7:**

> Hi [first name], this is Joey at GLP Home Options. Thank you for letting us help with your house. If you'd like to, you can share your honest experience on Google here: [link]. Whatever you share, good or not so good, is welcome, and no reply is needed. [Text only: Reply STOP to opt out.]

**The reminder (once):**

> Hi [first name], just a gentle follow-up in case the link got buried: [link]. No pressure at all. Thank you again.

**If someone does not use Google:**

> No problem. If you'd rather, you can leave it on our Facebook page: [link]. Or if a phone call is easier, I'm glad to walk you through it (I just can't write it for you).

**If a reply is a complaint:** thank them, say you will call, and call. Do not ask them to change or delete anything, and do not argue.

**Never say:** "five stars", "if you're happy", "mention [name]", "mention the quick close", anything about a gift, discount or entry, anything asking them to include a phone number or website, "please take it down" or "please update it".

Some customers will not have a Google account (it can use any email address, not only Gmail) or will not want one. Offering a phone call or the Facebook option is kinder than pressing for Google.

## The link

- Get it from the profile itself: Business Profile → Read reviews → "Get more reviews". Copy exactly what Google gives you. The site's builder only accepts a full https link on a Google address, so a typo cannot send a thank-you card to the wrong place.
- **Test it on a real phone,** signed in and signed out of Google, and confirm it opens the review box for GLP and not another business. A brand-new listing can take days before the link works (one 2017 forum thread says so; no current source).
- Older guides use a `search.google.com/local/writereview?placeid=…` link. Only 2017–2020 pages document it, and I could not confirm in 2026 that it works for a business with a hidden address. Use the profile's own link first.
- I could not confirm the shape of Google's short review links (the `g.page/r/…/review` kind that many guides show) from a Google page; Google's QR page does not show it. The profile's own link is the one to trust.

## Replying to reviews

- Reply to every review, in your own words, within a day (never later than two). 89% of consumers in the 2026 survey expect owners to respond, and half are put off by templated replies. Replies are public. The profile must be verified first.
- **Never reveal** the price, address, closing date, the reason for the sale (inheritance, divorce, foreclosure, a move), or anything the reviewer did not say themselves. Do not confirm someone was a customer beyond thanking them for what they posted. Do not ask for personal or financial details in a reply.
- Do not argue, correct facts in public, or threaten legal action. Take it offline with a phone number or email.
- **ATTORNEY:** any review that looks defamatory, and what to say when a dispute is open.

Skeletons (write each reply fresh; these are only a starting point):

- **Positive:** "Thank you, [first name]. We're glad the process felt clear and unhurried, and we wish you all the best with what comes next. If you ever need anything, call us at [number]."
- **Mixed or negative (use the reviewer's own point only):** "Thank you for telling us, [first name]. We're sorry [the thing they said] fell short of what you expected, and we'd like to understand it and put it right where we can. Please call [Joey or Ethan] at [number] or email [address] so we can talk."
- **Cannot match a customer:** "Thank you for the feedback. We can't match this to a closing we handled and would like to look into it. Please contact us at [number] so we can learn more."
- **Never include:** a request to delete, a gift or discount, anything promotional, legal language, or details of the deal.

## A review you think is wrong

- **A real customer, harsh or unfair:** it stays. The reply and a phone call are the remedy.
- **Not from a customer, or it breaks Google's rules** (spam, a conflict of interest, off topic): Business Profile → Read reviews → Report. Google says review takes "several days", gives a one-time appeal for up to 10 reviews, and warns "do not report a review just because you disagree with it". Practitioners advise not counting on removal.
- **Never** offer anything to make a review go away, never pressure the writer, never threaten.
- **A good review that vanishes:** Google's automated filter sometimes hides real reviews. Report it through the Business Profile help form (issue "Missing reviews"); practitioners report about 5 to 7 business days and no guarantee.

## What the site does with reviews

- **Today: nothing.** No review page, no "What homeowners say" band, no stars, no counts, no review or rating data for search engines. This is deliberate: there is nothing real to show, and the site is still hidden from search.
- **Once real facts are typed into `tools/reviews_data.py`:**
  - A quiet page, `review.html` ("Share your experience"), appears. It is where a card, an email or a text points. It is not in the menu or the footer, not in `sitemap.xml`, and kept out of search even after launch. Everyone gets the same page and the same button; it offers Facebook and a phone call for people with no Google account, and it says nothing is given in return.
  - Home and About get a "What homeowners say" band: the average and count **only from `MIN_SHOWN` reviews up** (it is 5 now; Joey decides), the month it was read ("As of March 2027"), a link to read every review on Google, and up to three quotes marked as a selection ("A selection: 2 of our 12 Google reviews, quoted exactly").
  - A note someone sent us directly can be shown with their written permission, marked "Sent to us", even before there are enough Google reviews.
- **What never shows:** stars; review or rating markup for search engines (Google does not give star results for a business's reviews of itself, so the markup would earn nothing and add risk); anything from an owner, relative, employee or business partner; a name or town the person did not agree to show; a quote without a record of permission; an average that is out of date (the checks warn at 35 days and fail at 62).
- **A selection is labeled as one,** and the full list is one click away. We do not hide real negative reviews on Google, and we do not pretend the quotes are all of them.

## Turning it on (for Jeremy; in this order)

Before you start: the Google Business Profile exists and is verified (see `research/gbp-setup-sheet.md`), the review link has been tested on a phone, and there is at least one real review.

1. Open `tools/reviews_data.py`. Type the tested link into `GOOGLE_REVIEW_URL`. That alone builds the quiet review page (no band, no numbers).
2. Once the public listing shows at least `MIN_SHOWN` reviews: type `GOOGLE_PROFILE_URL`, then `GOOGLE_RATING` and `GOOGLE_COUNT` **exactly as the listing shows them today**, and today's date in `CHECKED`. Never round up or type from memory.
3. For each quote you want on the site: copy the words exactly (shorten only with a visible "…"), give the month, the source ("Google", "Facebook" or "Direct"), a name or town **only if that person said yes**, `insider=False`, and a `permission` line saying what you hold (for a Google review, "public review; screenshot saved on [date] in [where]"; for a direct note, their written yes). Save the screenshot or the text reply with the closing records.
4. Run `python3 tools/reviews-check.py`. It must end with `FAILS 0`. It also builds a throwaway demo to prove the review page and the band still look and behave right.
5. Run `python3 tools/build-pages.py`. It refuses to build, with a plain message, if a fact breaks a rule.
6. Run `python3 tools/claims-check.py`. **It will fail on purpose:** a rating or a review count shown to visitors is a claim a person must say yes to (it also stops at the word "stars"). It cannot tell a real quote from an invented one; that protection is the permission line in step 3 and your own look at the public review. For each phrase it lists, add one line to `tools/claims-allow.txt` in the form `page | phrase in lower case | who checked, when, and where the proof is`, for example `index.html | 4.8 on google | read off the listing on 2027-03-02 by Joey; screenshot in the closing folder`. The lines you will need are for the phrases shown on Home and About ("4.8 on google", "from 12 reviews", "of our 12 google reviews", with your real numbers) and one for `review.html | tap the stars | instructions to the reviewer; no rating is claimed`. Add the matching rows to `CONTENT_CLAIMS.md`.
7. Read the promise on the review page out loud: "We don't give anything in return for a review, and we never ask anyone to change or take one down." Joey must be able to keep it for every customer.
8. Run the other checks (`python3 tools/checkpoint.py`, `tools/seo-check.py`, `tools/check-links.py`), look at the two bands and the review page on a phone, then commit.
9. **Every month:** re-read the two numbers on the real listing, change `CHECKED`, rebuild. `python3 tools/reviews-check.py` warns after 35 days and fails after 62.

## What I could not confirm (so nobody relies on it)

- No source says how many reviews a new home-buying business gets, or how many it takes to rank; the numbers above are vendor rates and surveys.
- Whether a text review request is marketing or informational, and what consent it needs (attorney).
- Whether the profile's review link works for a business with a hidden address in 2026; whether a brand-new link needs days; Google's exact filter thresholds (it publishes none).
- Whether Google's April 2026 clause on staff quotas and scripted content reaches ordinary wording like "how did we do on [thing]"; practitioners disagree and Google has not clarified. This kit stays on the safe side: one neutral message for everyone.
- Tennessee's rules on using a customer's name or photo in advertising (one secondary source; see `research/joey-kit.md`, section 2).
