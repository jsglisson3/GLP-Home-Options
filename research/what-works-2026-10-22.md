# What works on a site like this: research notes (Oct 22, 2026)

**For:** Jeremy, Joey and Ethan, and any later session. **Companions:** `site-review-2026-10-22.md` (what to change on our own pages) and "Proposed plan v3" in `ROADMAP.md`. Research for the family, not legal advice. Nothing here is on the site.

## What this is, and how far to trust it

Three web-only research passes (A, B and C) and one cold read of our own site (D) were run on Oct 22, 2026:

- **A, what converts** on home-selling and cash-offer sites (23 sources).
- **B, trust and usability** for wary, older, stressed home sellers (23 sources).
- **C, the Chattanooga market** and who a local seller actually finds. It names other businesses, so it lives in a private file that is not published (`research/private/`, see the end of this note).
- **D, a cold read of our own site** by a helper who played four made-up first-time visitors and had not seen our notes. It is reported in `site-review-2026-10-22.md` (and in full in `research/private/D-cold-read.md`).

**Strong ground:** consumer-protection advice (AARP, the National Consumers League, a state attorney general, the Better Business Bureau), accessibility rules (WCAG 2.2) and usability studies (Nielsen Norman Group).

**Weak ground (read this twice):**

- We found **no study that tests which trust features raise sign-ups on "we buy houses" sites.** Where a point below is about conversion, it is advice and observation, not proof.
- Every conversion and review number is **vendor data** (companies that sell ads, landing pages or review tools). Many sources are old, and each one is flagged.
- Only **six brand or competitor pages** were read in depth, as text; no visual design was judged. Most local firms' own sites were not readable by our tool, so "we did not see it" never means "they do not have it."
- Page contents came through machine summaries. **Re-check any number, quote or phone line on the original page before it goes on the site.**

Two numbers were re-checked by hand for this note: AARP's July 28, 2026 page and Pew's 2026 phone figures (both below).

## The ten findings that should change our site most

| # | What the research says | What it means for GLP | How sure |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Fear is the job.** Consumer groups and state attorneys general name the same worries again and again: a low offer, pressure and deadlines, a contract that is hard to leave, hidden fees, a buyer who sells your contract to someone else, and signing away the deed. | Answer each one in plain words, with promises GLP keeps on **every** deal and nothing more. | High that these are the named worries. None of it was tested for sign-ups. |
| 2 | **The honest comparison is rare.** In what we read, no page showed a net sheet that can come out in favor of listing. Big brands admit the lower price in a footnote, an FAQ or a guide. | Our net sheet is the one thing we could not find elsewhere. Feature it on Home and Contact too, with the assumptions next to the number, and check that no default favors either side (review N27). Keep "trades some price for speed." | High on what the pages say. Effect untested. |
| 3 | **Say who the buyer is and how the company earns money.** AARP lists "sign a contract, then sell it to a third party without ever assuming ownership" as a tactic. The National Consumers League warns about junk fees. | A short box: who buys the house, what the seller pays, how an offer is set, how GLP earns. Today the site says "a fair cash offer" and never says how it is figured. Needs Joey's real answers. | High that it is the biggest unspoken question. Medium that explaining it lifts trust. |
| 4 | **Point to checks that do not depend on us:** an agent's opinion or an independent appraisal, a HUD-approved housing counselor, your own attorney, an independent title or closing company, a Google and BBB look-up. | A plain "What to ask any buyer, including us" checklist (a draft is in the private B report). With no reviews yet, this is the strongest honest proof available. | High that the advice is consistent. Effect untested. |
| 5 | **Real names, faces, address and phone are the cheapest proof there is.** Stanford's credibility guidelines (2002) and BBB advice say so. Eye-tracking (Nielsen Norman, 2010) shows people look at real team photos and skip stock people. Only 22% read About pages in full or mostly (2002 survey), so one About page cannot carry trust alone. | Full names (if Joey and Ethan agree), short true bios and the base or service area, repeated in every footer. | High that consumers are told to check. Medium that it lifts trust. Old. |
| 6 | **Phone first, not phone only.** Pew, fielded Jan 30 to Jun 18, 2026: 79% of U.S. adults 65 and over own a smartphone and 96% own a cellphone, so about 1 in 5 older adults has no smartphone. Independent and franchise buyer pages put the phone up front; app-style national brands hide it. | The number as visible text on every page, in the same place, including the laptop header. Keep the desktop layout as good as the phone one. Make the net sheet printable. | High (Pew, checked by hand). Medium for the pattern (six sites). |
| 7 | **Keep the first ask small and answer fast.** Fewer form fields is the consistent direction (vendor data plus checkout research). Fast first contact is supported by two old studies of company web leads (2007, vendor-run; 2011, Harvard Business Review, seen second-hand). Neither studied home sellers. | Name and phone first; address and email optional. Instant alerts to both founders. Promise only a reply time Joey can keep. Never print "5 minutes" or "100 times." | Medium on direction, low on size. |
| 8 | **Older visitors still fail on the basics:** tiny type, pale text, small tap targets, forms that reject dashes or spaces, error messages they cannot find (Nielsen Norman, 123 participants aged 65 and over, 2019). WCAG 2.2 sets 24 by 24 pixels as the minimum target. | We are ahead here (44-pixel targets, a text-size floor, zero axe violations). Keep it, and test with 3 to 5 real readers aged 60 and over. | High. Old. |
| 9 | **Reviews matter, and a new business starts at zero.** BrightLocal 2026 (vendor): 97% of people read local reviews and 47% would skip a business with fewer than 20. Northwestern's Spiegel Center: purchase likelihood peaks at 4.0 to 4.7 stars and falls toward a perfect 5.0 (product sales, 2017). | Show no stars, counts or badges until they are real. Start the honest review path now (`lead-response-playbook.md`, section 1). | Medium. Vendor data. |
| 10 | **Keep pages light and the first screen focused, and do not expect miracles.** Nielsen Norman (2018, desktop): 57% of viewing time is above the fold. Google (2017): slower mobile pages lose visitors. LocaliQ 2026 (vendor): about 3.7% of real-estate paid clicks become a lead of any kind (call, form, chat or email), 8.05% for home improvement. Nothing exists for a brand-new site with no reviews. | Count call taps, text taps and form sends separately for about 90 days and judge on our own numbers. | Medium to low. |

## The fears, and what a trustworthy page can say

The "do not claim" column protects Joey and Ethan: those words are what the consumer warnings and the attorneys general look for.

| Worry or red flag | A trustworthy page can say | Do not claim unless it is true on every deal |
|---|---|---|
| A low offer; the seller cannot tell what the house is worth | The net sheet, including when listing nets more. A plain line that cash offers are usually below the top market price, and why. Ways to check value (an agent's opinion, an independent appraisal). | "Top dollar," "fair market value," "best offer in town," "we beat any offer." |
| Pressure and expiring offers | "No deadline. Take the offer home. Show it to family, an attorney or an agent." How long an offer stays open, and why. | Countdown timers, "limited time." "Close in 7 days" as a promise (the site says "as little as 7, usually 7 to 14," which is a range). |
| A contract that is hard to leave | A one-page plain summary of the contract and what happens if the seller changes their mind before closing. | "No obligation" or "cancel any time" about the **contract**, unless the paperwork says exactly that. (Our "no obligation" is about the first conversation and the offer; keep it that narrow.) AARP's model law would give a right to cancel until the deed transfers; that is a proposal, not today's rule. |
| The contract is sold to someone else | A clear statement of whether GLP buys with its own money and closes in its own name; if a partner or lender is involved, say so. | "We are direct buyers" or "we never assign contracts" if either has ever been, or could be, untrue. |
| Hidden fees; "we cover the closing costs" | An itemized "What it costs you" list and a sample closing statement marked "example." | "No fees," "no closing costs," "no commissions" unless every closing statement matches. |
| Paying before closing | "We never ask you for money before closing." Closing at an independent title company or attorney. | Naming a title company or attorney GLP has not actually engaged. |
| The offer drops after the inspection | How an offer is set and what could change it, and a promise that any change is explained in writing before the seller signs. | "Guaranteed offer" or "the price never changes." |
| Foreclosure "rescue" and signing over the deed | On a behind-on-payments page, first: call your lender, and a free HUD-approved counselor. | "Stop foreclosure," "save your home," any guarantee, or any hint not to call the lender. |
| Targeting grieving or older owners | "Bring someone you trust to every call." "Have your attorney read our paperwork." A calm line that GLP does not keep chasing after a no. | "We specialize in seniors, estates or probate" as a skill claim. "Trusted by hundreds of families." |
| Cannot verify the company | Legal name, address or service area, phone, email, hours, named founders with photos, and links to Google and BBB profiles once they exist. | "Licensed," "bonded," "insured," "accredited," "A+ rated," "award-winning" without a document in hand. |
| No reviews | "How to look us up." A line such as "This website is new; here is how to check us." Real, permissioned seller stories later. | Stock testimonials, invented names, star badges, "hundreds of homes." |

## What the competitors' pages have in common

(Names, ratings and page details are in the private file.)

- **The same promises everywhere:** no fees or closing costs, no repairs, any condition, a free no-obligation offer, closing in about 7 to 10 days. Several write speed and fees as absolutes and add a note in the fine print that timing is not guaranteed.
- **The same call to action,** repeated ("get a cash offer"), and a short 3 to 4 step process.
- **Proof is mostly review counts on other platforms.** Vendor lists show about 57 to 117 combined reviews for mid-sized local investors, and single digits for small ones. Named owners with photos are common, so "family-run" alone does not set a firm apart.
- **Things we did not see on any page we could read:** a worked example of what a seller nets, an explanation of how the offer is figured, or a page that says listing or keeping the house may be better.
- **A national ranking site is usually the first thing a local seller finds,** not a local buyer. Those lists earn money from the outcome, so their ratings are not neutral. Plan for the second visit: someone who has heard of GLP and is checking whether it is real.
- **The spam signals visitors already distrust:** unsourced counts ("1,300 homes," "a home bought every 20 minutes"), press-logo strips that link to unrelated articles, city names repeated in every heading, an undefined "fair cash offer," urgency with no real deadline, and autoplay video. We use none of these, with **one exception: "a fair cash offer" is not yet explained anywhere.** The "how we set an offer" box in finding 3 fixes that.

## Calls to action, forms and reply speed

- **Click-to-call, sticky bars, video, chat, pop-ups, trust badges and FAQs:** no independent conversion tests were found for any of them. Everything we do here is judgment plus what trustworthy pages do. Our bottom bar on phones, tap-to-call and tap-to-text links, no chat and no pop-ups all fit that.
- **Form length:** vendor data says real-estate forms usually have 4 to 5 fields and that fewer is better; checkout research (Baymard, e-commerce) agrees. Our form is the third-party GoHighLevel one; its required fields (phone and email today) are Joey's and Jeremy's to change.
- **Address first or last:** no test found. Instant-estimate tools ask for the address first because it feeds an estimate. GLP's offers come from a conversation, so name and phone first is the lower-friction choice (judgment).
- **Reply speed:** the safe sentence for internal use is "research on company web leads (2007 and 2011) found that the odds of reaching someone fall steeply in the first hour, so we reply fast and personally; nobody has measured this for home sellers, so it is a goal, not a promise or a number."
- **Say what happens next** before the form, and why each question is asked (done on Contact: "What happens next?").

## Writing for someone in a hard moment

| Do | Do not |
|---|---|
| Name the situation directly ("If a parent has died and you now own the house...") | Reach for soft stand-ins; euphemisms are harder to read, especially in a second language |
| Open with what the reader can do first and what they do not have to do yet ("You do not have to decide this week") | Open with a pitch or urgency ("Sell now before it's too late") |
| Say before the form what you will ask and what happens next | A vague "Get my offer!" button |
| Lay out the other paths, including ones that do not involve GLP | Suggest GLP is the only way out, or call the house a burden |
| On a behind-on-payments page, send readers first to their lender and a free HUD-approved counselor | Promise to stop foreclosure or guarantee any outcome |
| Use "you" and "we," short sentences and everyday words; explain probate, equity, lien and title once where they appear | Use investor words like "distressed," "motivated seller" or "ugly house" |
| Acknowledge the situation once, steadily, then get practical | Stack condolence lines, exclamation marks or jokes |
| Offer a low-pressure human step ("Call to ask questions; you do not have to take an offer," only if true), and invite them to bring someone they trust | Make the only action "Get my cash offer now" |

The grief and stress evidence is thin (a UK government practitioner blog and one nine-person study), and the divorce wording rule has no source; both are judgment.

## Older and stressed visitors: working targets

| Area | Target | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Reading level | About grade 6 to 8, then test with 3 to 5 readers aged 60 and over who say it back in their own words. Federal plain-language guidelines set no grade target; the range is our judgment. Our pages measure about grade 4 on our own simple yardstick, legal pages about 7.6. | Federal Plain Language Guidelines; judgment |
| Headings | Question headings in the visitor's words; paragraphs of about 150 words or fewer; an "On this page" list on long pages | Federal Plain Language Guidelines |
| Type | At least 16 CSS pixels; 18 or more is better; dark text on a light background; the page zooms without breaking | Nielsen Norman; 18 is judgment |
| Tap targets | At least 24 by 24 pixels (WCAG 2.2 AA); ours are 44 or more | W3C |
| Forms | Accept any phone format; plain error text next to the field; never ask for the same thing twice | Nielsen Norman; W3C |
| Calculator | Dollar signs and commas accepted; no drag-only controls; result shown as text; printable so a relative or executor can review it | W3C; printing is judgment |
| Phone | Number as visible text and a tap-to-call button on every page, in the same place; say who answers | Pew; Nielsen Norman |

## What results to expect

- Vendor figures for paid search: real estate 3.70%, home and home improvement 8.05% of clicks become a lead of any kind (LocaliQ, updated Jun 1, 2026). An older landing-page report (2017) put top dedicated real-estate pages at 11.2%. These differ in era, page type and what counts as a conversion; do not stack them.
- There is **no benchmark for a new, eight-page site with no reviews** and no figures for organic, direct or mailed traffic.
- Track **call taps, text taps and form sends separately.** A tap is not a connected call, and none of them is a closed deal. Judge after about 90 days of our own data.

## What we could not find

- No test of address-first forms, click-to-call, sticky bars, video, chat, pop-ups, trust badges, FAQs or review placement on lead pages.
- No study of what grief or stress does to reading and decisions, beyond one small paper and one practitioner blog.
- No federal or Tennessee consumer alert aimed at "we buy houses" companies surfaced in our searches (the Tennessee Attorney General page found is about mortgage-rescue scams and dates from 2019). The AARP page (checked by hand) says some states have passed related laws and proposes a model act; it is not Tennessee law.
- Chattanooga competitors' own sites, forms and About pages were mostly unreadable to our tool. Opening each by hand would finish that part.
- Google's own ranking, ads and map results for Chattanooga searches (our search tool is not Google). Parked with the rest of the search work.
